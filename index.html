Francisco Richter Mendoza

What can a partial record tell us about the process that produced it?

Research Publications Talks Teaching Contact

I am a scientific collaborator and lecturer at the Faculty of Informatics of the Università della Svizzera italiana in Lugano. I work on statistical inference for systems we only see in part: how species form and disappear, judged from trees of the species alive today; which relationships in data still hold when conditions change; and the same questions in astronomy, microbial ecology, public health and education.

I did my PhD at the University of Groningen (2021), supervised jointly by Ernst Wit and Rampal Etienne, after training as a mathematical engineer at the Universidad Técnica Federico Santa María in Valparaíso. Before that I worked on observation scheduling for ALMA, on photometric correction for ESO's Very Large Telescope, and on risk models in industry.

Research

Evolutionary biology

Trees of living species are incomplete twice over: the species that went extinct left no tip, and many living species were never sampled. I work on this along two lines that feed each other.

The first is statistical. Fitting a diversification model to a tree of living species means reconstructing what is not there. The method I developed does this by simulation: it fills in the missing lineages and fits the model on the completed trees. I am also working out how much of what the field reports about speciation and extinction survives once you admit that you do not know how many species a tree is missing.

The second is biological: what actually drives diversification. Each thing these models can read is a hypothesis biologists already argue about — whether diversity limits itself, whether a lineage's age changes its chances, whether evolutionarily distinct lineages are relicts on their way out, whether a fast-diversifying lineage passes that rate to its descendants. A question from my thesis runs through it: whether what limits diversification is the number of species or the amount of evolutionary history already present.

Robust and causal regularization

With Ernst Wit I work on which relationships in data still hold when conditions change — causes rather than correlations — in data where nobody recorded which conditions changed. I apply it where that question matters: to how academic and socioemotional learning are linked in Peru's national assessment, which I present at the Conference on Complex Systems in October 2026, and to problems in social and network science.

The same problem elsewhere

A system seen only in part, and not at random, keeps appearing in other fields. Stellar chemical abundances are now being arranged into trees, and I am asking what such a tree can and cannot tell us about the history of the Galaxy. I am also working on microbiome interaction networks and on the geography of injury mortality in Chile.
